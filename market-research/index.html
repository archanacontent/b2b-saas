Market Research And Opportunity

Many launch plans start with the product and work outward. The pitch, the price and the campaign calendar all get decided before anyone has confirmed which buyers need the product and what they would pay for it. Weeks later, the demos fill with poor-fit companies, and the sales team spends its time on deals that were never going to close.

Market research reverses that order. You start with the buyer's problem, measure how many companies have it and test whether they would pay to solve it. Positioning, pricing and launch content then rest on evidence the whole team can check.

You'll leave with a market brief, an opportunity scorecard that shows whether to proceed, and a checklist for testing demand before you commit budget.

Market Brief

A market brief puts the case for the launch on one page. If a VP asks why this segment, or a new hire asks who the buyer is, the brief gives both answers with a source behind each. Keep it short enough to read in five minutes, and date every figure so nobody acts on numbers that went stale months ago.

Market definition

One sentence naming the category, the buyer and the company size you're selling to.

Market size

A count of companies that fit, plus the spend you can realistically reach, each with a dated source.

Buyer problem

The pain as buyers describe it, quoted from reviews, sales calls or surveys.

Current alternatives

Everything buyers use today, spreadsheets and doing nothing included.

Trends

Changes expected in the next 24 months that will raise or lower demand, each one sourced.

Barriers to entry

Switching costs, strong incumbents or missing channels that will slow you down.

Open questions

Gaps the team still has to close, with an owner and a date for each.

Opportunity Scorecard

Weak opportunities usually get funded on enthusiasm. A weighted scorecard makes the team rate each factor on its own and put evidence next to every score.

Criterion Weight Score (1–5) Evidence
Pain severity 25%
Reachable market 20%
Willingness to pay 20%
Competitive gap 15%
Fit with current product 10%
Time to revenue 10%

Score any criterion without evidence as 1, however confident the team feels. A total under 3 sends the idea back for more research. Between 3 and 3.5, it moves to validation, and anything higher is ready for positioning work.

Demand Validation Checklist

Desk research can show that a problem exists, but only buyers can confirm they would pay to fix it. Run these checks before any launch budget is committed.

  • Buyer interviews - Hold at least 20 conversations inside the target segment, using the same core questions so the answers can be compared.
  • Review mining - Read 50 or more reviews of the tools buyers use now, and tag each complaint by theme.
  • Search demand - Check volume for the words buyers use to describe the problem, which are rarely the team's internal terms.
  • Price test - Put a real price in front of at least five qualified buyers and record how each reacts.
  • No-go condition - Agree in writing, before research begins, on the one finding that would stop the launch.

Worked Example

The company and its scores are fictional. Market data is real and linked.

A business-software suite with CRM, invoicing, help desk and HR apps wants to launch its CRM, now with a built-in AI assistant, for companies with 10 to 200 employees in a new market.

Market Brief

Market definition

CRM with a built-in AI assistant for companies with 10 to 200 employees, sold self-serve with inside sales support.

Market size

Pending. The team will count firms in the 10 to 200 band from the official business census before scoring reach.

Buyer problem

Software often disappoints once setup begins. In Capterra's 2026 Software Buying Trends survey of 3,385 decision-makers across 11 countries, 66% reported unexpected disruption, regret or both after a purchase. Of those with regret, 89% first hit a problem during implementation.

Current alternatives

Spreadsheets and shared inboxes, free CRM tiers, and enterprise CRMs built for much larger sales teams. (Team assumption, to be tested in interviews.)

Trends

Half the buyers in the same survey named AI as the top outside factor shaping their business goals.

Barriers to entry

Moving years of contact history is costly, and established CRMs have strong brand recall.

Open questions

The firm count for the target band, the industries feeling the most pain and a price that holds up next to free tiers.

Opportunity Scorecard

Illustrative scores.

Criterion Weight Score Evidence
Pain severity 25% 4 Capterra data on failed setups
Reachable market 20% 3 Firm count still pending
Willingness to pay 20% 1 No price test yet
Competitive gap 15% 3 Current CRMs are built for larger teams
Fit with current product 10% 5 The CRM already sells in other markets
Time to revenue 10% 3 Self-serve is possible, with sales help

The total is 3.05 out of 5, so the idea moves to validation. Pricing scored 1 for lack of evidence, which puts a price test at the top of the to-do list.

Mistakes To Avoid

  • Borrowed market sizes - A headline figure from an industry report rarely matches the companies your team can reach and sell to.
  • Free sign-ups read as demand - Interest in a free tier proves curiosity, and many of those users will never pay.
  • Research with no exit - If the team never sets a no-go condition, every finding ends up read as a reason to go ahead.
  • A buyer copied from another market - An ICP that worked elsewhere needs fresh interviews, because budgets and buying habits change between segments.

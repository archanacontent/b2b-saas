Demand Generation Support

Product marketing and demand generation often run on separate briefs. The growth team builds campaigns around whatever converts, the launch messaging sits in a document nobody opens, and the leads that arrive expect a different product from the one sales demonstrates. Conversion from lead to opportunity drops, and each team blames the other's numbers.

Product marketing's job here is to supply the substance, while demand generation runs the machine. Every campaign starts from the positioning, the persona pains and the proof you've already built. Your role is to keep each channel's message tied to that source.

You'll leave with a campaign brief and a set of channel plays for webinars, events, email, outbound and LinkedIn.

Campaign Brief

The brief is the hand-off between product marketing and the growth team. It should fit on one page and answer every question a campaign manager would otherwise have to chase you for.

Objective

The one result the campaign must deliver, as a number.

Audience

ICP segment and persona, with any buying trigger to target.

Core message

The headline promise and the pillar this campaign leads with.

Offer

What the buyer gets for responding, such as a trial, guide or demo.

Channels

Where the campaign runs, and the job of each channel.

Proof

The evidence the campaign can use, cleared for external use.

Budget and timing

Spend, start date and length.

Success measures

Leading indicators and the outcome metric, with targets.

Channel Plays

Each channel suits a different stage of the buyer's decision. Match the offer to that stage, so an early-stage reader isn't asked to book a demo they aren't ready for.

Channel Best used for What product marketing supplies
Webinars Showing the product working on a real problem The story, the demo flow and answers to likely questions
Events Meeting buyers who are actively comparing vendors Booth messaging, a one-pager and a short talk track
Email Moving known contacts toward a trial or demo Persona-specific copy and the proof for each email
Outbound Reaching companies that match a buying trigger Opening lines tied to the trigger, plus objection answers
LinkedIn Building awareness with the buying committee Problem-led posts and the data points behind them

Worked Example

The company and campaign are fictional and illustrative.

Campaign Brief: "The Missed Follow-Up"

Objective

300 trial sign-ups from ICP companies in the first 60 days.

Audience

Heads of sales at firms with three to 25 salespeople, triggered by hiring a new sales manager.

Core message

Every deal, from first call to paid invoice, in one CRM your team will use. Leads with the assistant pillar.

Offer

A 14-day trial with a guided import of their own pipeline.

Channels

LinkedIn for reach, outbound for trigger-matched firms, email for nurture and a monthly webinar.

Proof

Beta results on hours saved per rep, once confirmed.

Budget and timing

Eight weeks, starting on launch day.

Success measures

Trial sign-ups and trial-to-paid conversion, with a weekly check on cost per trial.

Three-Email Launch Sequence

Email Subject line Purpose
1 "The follow-up that never went out" Name the problem and its cost
2 "What your reps do after 5 p.m." Show the assistant removing admin
3 "Your pipeline, imported by Friday" Make the trial feel quick and low-risk

Mistakes To Avoid

  • Campaigns built around the offer alone - A discount with no problem attached attracts buyers who leave at renewal.
  • The same message in every channel - A LinkedIn post and a demo invitation need different asks, even when the positioning is shared.
  • Measuring leads instead of pipeline - A campaign that fills the form count but not the sales calendar has missed its objective.
  • No feedback from sales - Without reps reporting lead quality, the team keeps paying for the wrong audience.
